It is a big surprise to me that the frontline contender for my 2026 Game of the Year is neither the newest Resident Evil game, nor is it the James Bond game made by the developers of Hitman. Both of those seemed like they were going to be absolute slam dunks for the award. And don’t get me wrong, they’re great games - but they’re no Big Walk. 

<hr>

Big Walk is a co-op puzzle adventure game by Melbourne devs House House, who you may know for Untitled Goose Game. In the game, you walk around the bush with your friends and solve a variety of puzzles that are scattered across the world. I don’t want to expand on the particulars of the puzzles or the map itself - the game really excels at providing all these different “aha that’s how we solve that!” and “oh that’s how that works!” moments, so I think everyone should go in as blind as they can. In many ways, it gives me the same feelings that Outer Wilds did, which is very high praise. I will make some references to stuff you can see in the trailer for the game, but that’s the extent of what I’ll be going into spoiler territory wise.

If I don’t want to talk about the puzzles in a puzzle adventure game, I clearly want to talk about the adventure side of things. Big Walk is a game that embraces the idea that it's about the journey, not the destination. The player movement speed isn’t particularly fast, and the unlockable train and chair lift around the island are focused more around ease of transport as opposed to being a quick travel option. You never feel rushed or that the game is trying to intentionally point you towards any particular direction. You’re easily able to take in the beautiful landscape, which is modelled after Victoria’s own Wilson’s Prom, as you explore. Combined with a very tight proximity chat radius (meaning you can only hear people talk via microphone when they’re close to you), it results in spending a lot of downtime with your fellow players where you’re going to be chatting like you would if you were on a normal hike. I played through this entire game with my close friends Chris and Ryan. Despite the fact we talk every day, the novelty of doing it while on a digital walk in bird avatars was still very effective and our dumb chatter really helped sell the experience. 

I enjoyed this downtime and minutiae of walking around as much, if sometimes even more than, the solving of puzzles. A big driving force behind that is that I decided to be our little group’s “photographer” who documented our adventure by taking lots of pictures, much like the role I play in real life when I’m hanging out with friends. Big Walk doesn’t have a dedicated photo mode, but you’re able to disable all the user interface stuff and take clean screenshots. I actually used them

I think the fact that I was able to do this and have it fit within the gameplay loop goes to show how well designed Big Walk is, and how it has such a clear identity of its own. There are a slew of co-op games that flood the market these days, aptly nicknamed “friendslop”. So many of those games are fun but ultimately disposable experiences. They’re all slight variations on the same idea - randomised levels that you can play endlessly, and gameplay mechanics which let you fuck over the people you play with for comedy’s sake. 

Big Walk is the antithesis to these types of games. You *need* to meaningfully engage with the world and the people you play with. You *need* to communicate and work together to solve puzzles. If someone isn’t invested in the experience and instead is trying to mess with you, you can’t progress and that would suck! There is a definitive end and that is what you’re working towards. Big Walk rewards you when you buy in fully, and I think my short lived game photographer career really reflects that.

<hr>

Looking back through my photos as I write up this blog post, I’m already feeling nostalgic for the Big Walk and jealous of everyone who gets to play this for the first time. I wish I could solve those puzzles again. I wish I could feel the rush of riding the chair lift for the first time. It’s a truly special game to me, and I hope that you get to enjoy it as much as I did.
